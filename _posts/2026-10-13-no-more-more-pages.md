## This post exists because of something I saw on an infinite feed

I found this one the way you'd expect: scrolling LinkedIn. Someone had written a post about Aza Raskin, the designer who is usually credited with inventing infinite scroll, and how he has come to [regret it](https://mastersofscale.com/video/he-invented-infinite-scroll-and-says-he-regrets-it/). By his own back-of-the-envelope math, the feature burns through hundreds of thousands of human lifetimes of attention. I read that on an infinite feed and then followed a link back to 2006.

The link went to a short post on 37signals' *Signal vs. Noise* blog titled ["No more 'more' pages?"](https://signalvnoise.com/archives2/no_more_more_pages). It's about Humanized Reader, a demo from Raskin's company at the time, which loaded more content automatically as you neared the bottom of the page. Their principle was "Don't force the user to ask for more content: just give it to them." The post's author liked the idea and named the two obvious problems: the scrollbar stops telling you anything, and finding your way back to something you already saw gets hard.

In 2006 I was in high school. I wasn't into software yet and I definitely wasn't reading 37signals, but I was on the internet. I had Gmail and Facebook, and those were important platforms to me. I had no idea that a few people in a comment section were arguing over the shape the rest of my online life would take.

## Twenty years of the same argument

The post has 64 comments, and reading them now is uncanny. One person says the scrollbar becomes useless. Another says it's fine for a newsreader but wrong for search, where you're looking for something specific. Others say it breaks without JavaScript, for keyboard users, and on phones. Someone proposes a "more" link instead of auto-loading. Someone asks how a search engine would place ads on a page that never ends. And Raskin himself shows up in the thread to say that user testing found most of the expected problems weren't serious, and that his team was working on visual landmarks to help.

No single comment stood out to me. What stood out is that it's the same debate we're still having. Every one of those objections is still true, and infinite scroll is everywhere anyway.

That makes me wonder how much debate actually affects implementation. The arguments were made, clearly and early, by people who cared about design. It didn't matter. The ad question got answered, and the answer was that an endless page is a great place to sell ads. When the money points one way, it seems to override everything else.

I don't think infinite scroll is ever really the right call. I also don't think it's ever going away. I just wish there were fewer feeds in my life.

## Feeds that end

Not all feeds feel the same to me, though, and I've been trying to pin down why.

Letterboxd has a feed. So does Discord. Neither one bothers me the way Twitter, Bluesky, TikTok, Reddit, Instagram, Threads, or Facebook do. The difference is that both are still chronological.

On Letterboxd, new activity is at the top and history runs down the page. When you reach the bottom there's a button: *Load more activity*. You can keep going, but you have to ask, and eventually you run into things you've already seen. That's the ending. The feed is telling you that you're caught up.

Discord works the same way upside down. New messages are at the bottom and history runs up. Scroll far enough and you're back to where you left off yesterday.

An algorithmic feed has no "already seen." There's always something new because new is the whole point. I've lost my place in feeds like that plenty of times. It's annoying, but I get over it.

## Every frame

I don't use feed blockers or screen time reminders. As friction, they don't work for me. I tap through the warning and keep going. What worked was going after my own habits, and that started with movies.

Movies have always been where I go to pay attention. I remember watching *The Prestige* and locking in from the opening scene, telling myself I was going to figure it out before the end. I didn't, even though I was paying close attention. That's when I understood what it takes to watch, and enjoy, a movie built like a puzzle box. Directors love making those, and I love being on the other side of them.

As phones became part of everything, I noticed they were wearing down my attention span. When the pandemic started, I really got into movies, and they became a safe place to practice what I think of as attention span expansion. Phones go away. I sit and focus on one thing. When a movie is compelling, I want to watch every frame and experience every moment as fully as I can. That's where my attention is. That's what deserves my thoughts.

I watch a lot at home, at friends' houses whenever they invite me, and in theaters. The theater is the ultimate feed blocker: dark room, big screen, a fixed running time, everyone agreeing to look in the same direction for two hours. At least that's how it's supposed to work. Over the last year or so I've had more rude interactions in theaters than in years past, almost always about phones. I think that's a societal thing more than a theater thing. The phones are really a problem.

Streaming services have brought infinite scroll to video, and the clearest example is autoplay. Some services start the next episode before I've decided I want it. Some do it after movies too. That ruins one of my favorite parts of the experience: watching the credits.

I sit through the credits partly out of respect for the filmmakers, but mostly because I like reading them. Sometimes I spot a name I know, someone I'm a fan of, who worked on the film below the line and nowhere near the poster. Sometimes I see something I want to look up later. And directors often save a great song for the end. You don't get any of that if you don't stay for the credits. So when the credits start and I get less than ten seconds before the service decides to show me something else, it drives me up the wall. The story is over, but the movie isn't. Let it end.

## The consequences of my thoughts

A movie deserves my thoughts *and the consequences of those thoughts*.

A movie usually leaves me with questions. I want to answer them, either on my own or with other people. The thinking keeps going after the credits, but it's about one contained story with a beginning, a middle, and an end.

A feed doesn't leave me with questions. What I feel after a feed binge is closer to illness. The posts hop everywhere. One second you're looking at a genocide, and the next it's naked famous people selling perfume. There's no story to hold onto, so the consequences of whatever I thought have nowhere to go. The difference between a feed and a movie isn't just how much time each one takes. It's the quality of what each one leaves in my head.

That's also why I log everything on Letterboxd. I keep a diary of every film I watch, including rewatches. A review is where I write down what I thought, and sometimes how I got there and how the film made me feel. Logging and rating is how I close a film out, and I never do it until the credits roll. Whenever something isn't on Letterboxd I usually add it, which I've done for a few local filmmakers in Jackson. If you have a film you want on Letterboxd and need help getting it there, reach out to me.

## A book I chose

Movies don't fit into a workday, so books took over those in-between moments. I carry a book everywhere. When I have five or ten minutes, instead of pulling out my phone, I read. Right now it's John Green's *Hollywood, Ending* for book club.

You could argue that a book read in five-minute chunks isn't so different from a feed read in five-minute chunks. To me it's very different, and not just because of continuity or authorial intent. It's the fact that I'm holding a book. A series of choices I made earlier is the reason that book is in my hand. I prepared it for myself. A feed has its own agenda. A book has the one I brought to it.

At home I keep my phones in one place and try to do one thing at a time: chores, a movie, hanging out with my cat, Olivia. The last thing I want is to spend a whole night on the couch scrolling Instagram. Honestly, that's so boring.

## The LinkedIn I'd keep

If I could drop one feed tomorrow with no social or professional cost, it would be LinkedIn. The irony that LinkedIn is also where I found the post that started all this isn't lost on me.

The version of LinkedIn I actually like is the [C Spire Gaming Discord server](https://discord.gg/cspiregaming). It's mostly C Spire employees who love games and a long tail of other niche interests. Every so often someone new shows up and we bring them into the fold. We talk about gaming news and cross-post things from Bluesky, Instagram, and Threads, and even the occasional LinkedIn post, since LinkedIn embeds nicely in Discord. It's the same internet, filtered through people I know, in a channel that runs in order and has a bottom.

I think the same way about JXN Film Club. I want members to visit the site, see what's new, and then leave to go find someone to watch a movie with, or sit down and watch one on their own. The whole point of the site is to connect people over movies, and I don't want it standing in the way. If those meetups go well, people will come back on their own. That's the kind of return visit I want, not the kind a feed manufactures.

## No ending

I don't have a neat conclusion. My opinion of infinite feeds is pretty negative, and there isn't much I can do about them beyond deciding where my own attention goes. I accept them as part of being a citizen of the internet right now. As for whether debate ever beats incentives, I don't know. The 2006 comment section suggests it doesn't, but I'd like to be wrong.

What I keep coming back to is that the 2006 post was asking whether we should get rid of "more" pages, the moment where the page ends and you decide whether to keep going. Twenty years later, that moment is what I miss most. So I make my own: a book in my bag, a phone in another room, and a movie that runs all the way through the credits.
